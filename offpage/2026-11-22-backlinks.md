### The article (original; not published anywhere)

**Specifying geotextile on Indian projects: why the GSM is not enough**

*By Ranjeet Sharma, Kiran Nonwovens, Jaipur*

Walk onto almost any Indian road or drainage site where a nonwoven geotextile is being laid and
ask what fabric was ordered. The answer is nearly always a number: "two hundred GSM", "three
hundred GSM". Mass per unit area has become the shorthand for geotextile in this country. It is
easy to say, easy to check with a scale, and easy to compare between quotations. It is also the
reason many geotextile disputes start.

**What GSM does and does not tell you**

GSM tells you how much fibre is in a square metre of fabric. It does not tell you how that fibre
behaves. Two needle punched geotextiles of the same weight can differ widely in strength, in how
much they stretch before failing, in how well they resist a stone pressed into them, and in how
freely water passes through them. The polymer matters (polypropylene and polyester behave
differently), so does the fineness of the fibre, how densely the web was needled, and whether the
fabric was heat-treated. A buyer comparing two "300 GSM" quotations on price alone may be
comparing two quite different products.

The site does not care about weight either. A separation layer under a haul road has to survive
crushed stone being tipped and rolled onto it. A filter around a subsoil drain has to let water
through without letting the surrounding silt follow. A cushion under a pond liner has to stop a
sharp stone from reaching the membrane. Each of those jobs is described by a different property,
and none of them is GSM.

**The properties that describe the job**

Specifications written abroad, and many Indian design documents, describe a geotextile by four or
five measured properties:

- *Tensile strength and elongation*, measured either on a wide strip (reported in kN per metre of
  width) or by a grab test (reported in newtons). Nonwovens stretch a great deal before breaking,
  which lets them follow uneven ground without tearing.
- *Puncture resistance*, most often by the CBR test, in which a flat 50 mm plunger is pushed
  through a clamped sample. It is the closest laboratory stand-in for a stone pressed into the
  fabric by a roller.
- *Opening size*, which decides which soil particles are held back. It is found indirectly, by
  sieving particles through the fabric.
- *Permeability*, the ease with which water crosses the fabric, and for thicker grades the flow
  the fabric can carry along its own plane.

These are the numbers that relate to what the fabric must do. GSM remains useful as an identity
check, to confirm that the roll on site is the roll that was approved, but it is the result of a
specification, not a substitute for one.

**The same property, two different numbers**

A second source of confusion is the test method. International practice uses both ISO and ASTM
methods, and they are not interchangeable. Grab tensile and wide-width tensile results cannot be
converted into each other by a fixed factor. The ASTM apparent opening size and the ISO
characteristic opening size are both measures of pore size, but one is found by dry sieving glass
beads and the other by wet sieving soil, and they give different values on the same roll.
Permeability is reported as permittivity under ASTM and as a velocity index under ISO.

There is also the question of what a quoted value means. A datasheet may list typical values, the
average of production, while a specification written in the American style lists minimum average
roll values, which sit below the typical figure. A supplier's typical value and a tender's minimum
value can look similar and still not be comparable. When a test report is submitted, the method,
the direction of testing (along or across the roll), the number of specimens and the laboratory
all matter as much as the headline figure.

**A short checklist for a better specification**

Consultants and buyers who want fewer arguments at the delivery stage can ask for five things in
every geotextile specification:

1. The function of the layer: separation, filtration, drainage or protection.
2. Each required property with its test method named, for example "CBR puncture by ISO 12236".
3. Whether the values are minimums, minimum average roll values or typical values, and in which
   direction.
4. The polymer, if the design depends on it, for instance where the fabric will sit against
   lime-treated soil or fresh concrete.
5. How the fabric will be accepted on site: which tests, on how many rolls, and by which
   laboratory.

None of this is new. It is how geotextile is specified in most mature markets. Indian
infrastructure is laying geotextile at a scale that makes the habit worth adopting here as well.
A tender that says "300 GSM" invites the cheapest 300 GSM. A tender that states the strength,
puncture, opening size and permeability the design needs, with the methods named, gets the
fabric the design assumed.

**Q: What is the difference between woven and nonwoven geotextile?**

> They do different jobs. A woven geotextile is made on a loom from flat tapes or yarns; it is
> thin and strong in tension, so it is used for stabilisation and reinforcement. A nonwoven
> (usually needle punched) is a thick, felt-like fabric of entangled fibres; water passes
> through it and, in heavier grades, along it, so it is used for drainage, filtration,
> separation on wet ground and as a cushion under pond and canal liners. Comparing them by GSM
> misleads: a light woven can be stronger in tension than a heavy nonwoven, while the nonwoven
> drains far better. Decide by the job first. (I work with a nonwoven geotextile supplier, so
> I see this question from buyers every week; we wrote a longer comparison here:
> kirannonwovens.com/guides/woven-vs-nonwoven-geotextile)

**Q: How do I calculate how many rolls of geotextile I need?**

> Divide the area to cover by the area of one roll, after adding an allowance for overlaps and
> cutting. A 5.0 m × 50 m roll covers 250 m². If overlaps are 300 mm on a site, the allowance is
> often 10% or more. Example: 2,600 m² plus 10% is 2,860 m², which is 11.4, so 12 rolls. Roll
> weight is GSM × roll area: a 200 GSM roll of 250 m² weighs 50 kg (plus core and wrap). There is
> a free calculator that does both here: kirannonwovens.com/guides/geotextile-roll-weight-and-coverage

**Q: What GSM geotextile is used for road construction in India?**

> There is no single answer; the design and the tender set it. Lighter grades are common for
> separation over firm ground, heavier grades on soft or wet subgrades and under coarse,
> angular stone. More importantly, a good specification names the strength, puncture and
> permeability values the layer needs, not only the GSM, because two fabrics of the same weight
> can perform differently. Ask the project engineer for those values before ordering.
